The product page is the most visited part of a store and the most neglected. Ads point at it, search lands on it, and it usually consists of a photo, a price, a button and text copied from the manufacturer. Meanwhile the buyer is looking for answers to just four questions in the first few seconds, and if any one of them goes unanswered, they leave.

Here is what to change, and in what order.

## The buyer's four questions

**"Is this the thing I need?"** The name, key specs and photos must confirm it without scrolling.

**"Will it fit me?"** Size, compatibility, capacity, power - it varies by product, but the question is always there.

**"When will I get it and what will I pay in total?"** Not "delivery from 9 złoty", but a specific date and a final amount.

**"What if it does not work out?"** Returns, warranty, support.

Everything else on the page exists to serve those four questions.

## The first screen

What has to be visible without scrolling on mobile:

- **A photo** that can be enlarged.
- **The name** as a buyer would say it, not the SKU from your inventory system.
- **The price**, and the previous price if discounted.
- **Availability** and a **delivery date**: "arrives 3 September" works far better than "in stock".
- **The variant picker**, if there is one, with clear labels.
- **The add-to-cart button**, large and sticky while scrolling on mobile.

If a half-screen promo banner or a newsletter popup sits on the first screen, you are paying for ads and hiding the product yourself.

## Photography

The only element that substitutes for handling the product. The minimum:

- **5-8 images** instead of one.
- **Different angles** and close-ups of details.
- **Scale**: the product in someone's hands, in a room, next to a familiar object. The most common reason for returns is "it turned out smaller than I thought".
- **What is in the box** as its own shot.
- **A 15-30 second video** if the product is complex.

All images in modern formats and properly compressed, otherwise the conversion gain is eaten by the speed loss.

## Price and delivery with no surprises

The leading cause of abandoned carts is unexpected costs at the last step. What helps:

- **Show delivery cost on the product page**, not only in the cart.
- **A free delivery threshold** with a hint of how much is left to reach it.
- **A specific arrival date** that accounts for the current time: "order by 14:00 and we ship today".
- **Payment methods visible immediately**: BLIK, Przelewy24, card, cash on delivery, instalments. On setting those up, see [the payment gateway article](/en/blog/payment-gateway-poland-blik-przelewy24).
- **Pickup points**: for Poland, showing InPost on the product page is a strong argument - covered in [the integration article](/en/blog/inpost-paczkomaty-integration).

## A description people actually read

The rule is simple: **answer first, detail second**.

1. **Two or three sentences** on who the product is for and what problem it solves.
2. **Key specifications** as a list, most important first.
3. **Details**: materials, dimensions, what is included, care, compatibility.
4. **Answers to common questions** right on the page.

Copied manufacturer text works for nobody - not the buyer, not search, not AI assistants - because it is identical across a hundred stores. Why that matters for getting recommended is in [the agentic commerce article](/en/blog/agentic-commerce-chatgpt-shopping-store).

## Reviews

The most underrated element. What matters:

- **Next to the price** - rating and review count, visible immediately.
- **With customer photos**, if you can collect them.
- **With store replies**, especially to criticism. An answered negative review builds more trust than no negative reviews at all.
- **Without scrubbed criticism.** A flawless 5.0 across a hundred reviews reads as suspicious.
- **With `Review` and `AggregateRating` markup** for stars in search results.

If you have no reviews yet, everything that substitutes for them works: photos of real orders, a "bought N times" counter, use-case stories.

## Handling objections

A block worth keeping on every product page:

- **Return terms** in one line: "14 days to return, return shipping free".
- **Warranty** and what to do about a defect.
- **A size chart** or compatibility table - opening in place, not on a separate page.
- **A contact route** for pre-purchase questions. An [AI agent](/en/services/ai-agents) works well here: someone asks about sizing or compatibility and gets an answer in a second without leaving the page.

## Small things that kill orders

From what I see regularly when reviewing stores:

- The add-to-cart button scrolls away and has to be hunted for on a long page.
- Nothing happens visibly after adding to cart, so it is unclear whether it worked.
- Registration required before checkout.
- An order form with fields that are not needed for delivery.
- The variant selection resets when the page refreshes.
- The item is out of stock, but you only find out in the cart.
- Slow loading on mobile. Covered in [the site speed article](/en/blog/slow-website-core-web-vitals).

## What to test first

If you want to run experiments, start with the most sensitive spots:

1. A delivery date instead of an "in stock" label.
2. Showing delivery cost on the product page.
3. A sticky buy button on mobile.
4. The review block higher up the page.
5. Return terms next to the button.

Test one change at a time, for at least two weeks, and judge it by orders rather than clicks.

## FAQ

**What has to be on the first screen of a product page?**
An enlargeable photo, the name phrased the way a buyer would say it, the price, availability with a specific delivery date, the variant picker and a prominent add-to-cart button. On mobile the button should stay reachable while scrolling. Popups and large banners are removed from the first screen: they hide exactly the information the visitor came for.

**How many photos does a product page need?**
Five to eight at minimum: an overall view, different angles, close-ups of details, a shot that conveys scale (in hand, in a room, beside a familiar object) and an image of what is included. Scale matters most, because the most common reason for returns is a mismatch between expected and actual size. For complex products a short 15-30 second video helps.

**How should a product description be written so people read it?**
Answer first, detail second: two or three sentences on who it suits and what problem it solves, then key specifications as a list, then details and answers to common questions. Copied manufacturer text works for neither the buyer, nor search, nor AI assistants, because it is identical across a hundred other stores.

**Do reviews affect product page conversion?**
Noticeably, especially with customer photos and store replies. An answered negative review builds more trust than its absence: a perfect 5.0 across a large number of reviews looks suspicious. Show rating and review count next to the price rather than only at the bottom, and mark them up with Review and AggregateRating for stars in search results.

**Why do people add items to the cart and not buy?**
Most often because of unexpected costs at the last step: a delivery charge that was never shown on the product page, or forced registration. Other frequent causes are an awkward order form with unnecessary fields, a missing familiar payment method and unclear return terms. It is easy to verify: complete a purchase on your own phone as an ordinary customer and count the steps.
