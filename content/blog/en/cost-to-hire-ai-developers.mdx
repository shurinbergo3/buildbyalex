Short answer: independent AI developers in the EU charge **€50-90/hour**, agencies **€100-200/hour**, and a mid-level in-house AI engineer costs **€75,000-110,000** a year fully loaded. For a first project - one agent, one process - the independent route delivers the same output for roughly a third of the agency price. In-house starts making sense when AI work becomes continuous.

Below is the comparison written the way I'd want it if I were buying rather than selling.

## Rates by model, 2026

| Model | Rate | Typical first project | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent developer (EU) | €50-90/h | €1,500-4,000 | First agent, defined scope |
| Independent (US/UK) | $90-180/h | $4,000-12,000 | Same, at higher rates |
| Boutique agency | €100-160/h | €8,000-25,000 | Several parallel workstreams |
| Large agency | €150-250/h | €30,000+ | Enterprise procurement |
| Offshore team | €25-50/h | €3,000-10,000 | Volume work, defined specs |
| In-house engineer | €6,000-9,000/mo | salary + ramp-up | Continuous roadmap |

Most business AI projects - an agent on your documents, a workflow that reads incoming email, a qualification bot - are 40 to 120 hours of work. That range is what makes the hourly differences add up so quickly.

## What you actually pay for at agency rates

Not better engineers, usually. Overhead: account management, a project manager, sales cost, and the risk buffer for the person who eventually does the work being unavailable.

Sometimes that's worth it. If you need four workstreams running at once with deadlines you can enforce contractually, an agency provides capacity a single person can't.

What you should be aware of: the person who impressed you in the pitch is often not the person writing your code. Ask who specifically will build it and ask to speak with them.

## What you get from an independent developer

Direct contact with the person doing the work, faster decisions, no telephone game between you and the code.

The risk is concentration. One person means one calendar, one holiday, one illness. Reduce it the boring way: your repository, your API accounts, written documentation at handover. Then a second developer can continue - which is the actual definition of not being locked in.

I work this way myself, so treat that as a disclosed bias. The counterweight is that I'll say when a project needs a team - anything with parallel tracks and a fixed launch date usually does.

## The in-house math

A mid-level AI engineer in Poland costs €4,500-7,000 a month gross; in Western Europe €7,000-11,000. Fully loaded - taxes, equipment, tooling, management time - budget 30-40% on top.

First-year total: €75,000-160,000 depending on market.

That buys roughly 1,800 productive hours. The same money spent with an independent developer buys 900-1,400 hours with no ramp-up and no idle time between projects.

In-house wins when: AI work is continuous rather than project-shaped, the systems being touched are complex enough that context takes months to build, or data rules require everything to stay inside the company.

In-house loses when: you have one agent to build. A full-time engineer finishes it in six weeks and spends the rest of the year looking for work to do - which is how AI departments end up building internal tools nobody asked for.

## The hidden costs in every model

**Your time.** Every project needs someone internal answering questions, providing documents, testing. Budget 10-20% of the project length in your own hours. Projects stall on this more than on development.

**Data preparation.** If your knowledge lives in five inconsistent places, someone pays for consolidating it - either you in hours or the developer in fees.

**Running costs.** Tokens and hosting after launch, typically €20-150 a month. Not large, but it should be in the plan rather than a surprise. [Detailed breakdown here](/en/blog/ai-agent-maintenance-cost).

**Change budget.** A working agent generates ideas for the next one. Plan 10-20% of project value annually for changes - not because it breaks, but because your business moves.

## How to compare quotes that aren't comparable

Ask every candidate for the same three things: a fixed price for a defined first scope, an estimate of your monthly running cost, and their ownership terms.

The third one separates the field faster than price does. Repository in your organisation, API accounts in your name, copyright assigned - or not. A cheaper quote that keeps the code isn't cheaper, it's a subscription with an unclear price.

## FAQ

**How much does it cost to hire an AI developer per hour?**
€50-90 for independents in the EU, €100-200 at agencies, $90-180 for US and UK independents. Most business projects are quoted fixed-price instead, which is easier to compare and shifts estimation risk to the person who can actually manage it.

**Is offshore AI development worth it?**
It can be for well-specified work where you have someone internally able to review output. Where it usually disappoints is on projects needing constant clarification about your business process - the coordination cost eats the rate advantage, and the failure mode is a technically correct build of the wrong thing.

**When should I hire an in-house AI engineer?**
When there's at least a year of continuous AI work queued, or when your data rules prevent external access. For a single project, the first-year cost of a hire buys two to three times more delivered hours from an independent developer.

**What's a realistic budget for a first AI project?**
€1,500-4,000 for one agent or one automated process, delivered in two to six weeks. Budgets far above that at the start usually mean scope that hasn't been narrowed yet, which is the most expensive thing you can carry into a first project.

**How do I know if I'm overpaying?**
Compare the fixed price against the hours it plausibly takes: a knowledge-base agent with one channel is 40-70 hours of work. If a quote implies 300 hours for that scope, ask what the extra 230 cover - the answer is usually process, not product.
