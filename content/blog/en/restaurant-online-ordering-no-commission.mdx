You run a restaurant, the delivery orders keep dropping in through Glovo, Pyszne and Uber Eats, and on paper it looks like business is good. Then you count the month and see that on every delivered order the aggregator quietly took 20-30%, and on the cheaper dishes you basically cooked for free. Restaurant online ordering with no commission isn't a fantasy - it's your own ordering system or app, with online payments and an order landing straight in the kitchen, where you keep the full margin and own the customer instead of renting them from a platform.

## What the aggregators actually take

Glovo, Pyszne.pl and Uber Eats don't run a charity. The standard commission on a restaurant order sits around 25-30% of the order value, sometimes a little lower if you negotiate volume, often higher once you add the "extras" the platform pushes.

Put real numbers on it. A customer pays 80 zł for an order. At 28% commission the platform keeps about 22 zł before you've paid for a single gram of food. Now subtract food cost (say 30%, another 24 zł), the driver if you deliver yourself, the packaging, the staff time. On a 80 zł order you're often left with single-digit złoty - and on a 35 zł pizza-for-one you can finish in the red. Run 600 delivery orders a month at an average ticket of 70 zł and 27% commission, and you've handed the aggregator roughly 11,000 zł in a single month. Over a year that's well past 130,000 zł.

But the commission isn't even the worst part. The worst part is invisible: the person who ordered from you is not your customer, they're Glovo's customer. You don't have their phone number, you can't text them a Friday promo, you can't bring them back for free. Next week the app shows your kitchen next to four cheaper rivals, and you pay commission again for a guest who already loves your food.

## What you don't own on the aggregators

On the platforms you build turnover, not a business that's worth anything when you sell it. Three things stay on their side of the wall.

- **The customer data.** Names, phones, addresses, order history - all of it lives in the aggregator's account, not yours. No loyalty SMS, no "we miss you" coupon, no birthday offer.
- **The relationship.** The guest remembers "I ordered on Pyszne," not the name of your kitchen. You can't build loyalty on a logo that isn't yours.
- **The margin on repeat orders.** A regular who orders from you every Friday pays you commission every single Friday. On your own channel that same regular costs you almost nothing after the first order.

Your own ordering flips the math. The first order through your own system might cost a bit in ads, but the fifth, tenth and fiftieth from the same regular are nearly free - because you already hold their number and your own channel.

## Web ordering vs an app - which one you actually need

Both keep the margin. They differ in who you're keeping it from.

A **web ordering page** is the right start for almost everyone: nothing to install, it works from a Google or Instagram link or a QR code on the table and the flyer, and it indexes for searches like "pizza delivery [your district]." It's cheaper and faster to ship.

A **native app** earns its place when you have a real base of regulars and you want them to reorder in three taps, get push notifications about the Friday deal (push you don't pay 28% for), and collect loyalty stamps. The honest order is: launch the web ordering first, and add the app once your repeat orders justify it.
