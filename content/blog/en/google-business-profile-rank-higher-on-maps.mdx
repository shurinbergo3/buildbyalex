To rank higher on Google Maps you optimize the three things Google actually measures for the local pack: how close you sit to the person searching (**proximity**), how well your profile matches what they typed (**relevance**), and how known and trusted your business looks online (**prominence**). You can't move your address, but you control relevance and most of prominence - the right primary category, a complete profile, and a steady stream of reviews. Those levers, in that order, are what pushes a Google Business Profile up the map.

I set these up for local businesses in Poland, so here's the honest version - what moves the needle, what's a waste of time, and the mistakes that quietly get you filtered out of the results.

## How the local pack actually ranks

The "local pack" is the block of three businesses with the little map that shows up when someone searches "dentist near me" or "law firm Warsaw." Getting into those three spots is worth more than page one of the normal blue links, because that's where the high-intent clicks and calls go. Google ranks it on three factors, and it helps to know which ones you can touch.

| Factor | What it means | Can you control it | How you move it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proximity | How close you are to the searcher | No | A real address in the area you serve |
| Relevance | How well your profile matches the query | Yes | Correct primary category, complete info, services |
| Prominence | How known and trusted you look | Mostly | Reviews, NAP consistency, links, a real website |

**Proximity** is the one you can't game. Google shows results near the person searching, and that radius shrinks in dense cities. This is why your ranking looks different from your competitor's ranking down the street, and why there's no single "position 1" on Maps - it's personal to each searcher's location.

**Relevance** is where most people leave money on the table. Google needs to understand exactly what you do, and it reads that from your categories, your services, your description, and your reviews. A profile that just says "company" ranks for almost nothing.

**Prominence** is your reputation signal: review count and rating, how consistent your details are across the web, links pointing at your site, and how established the business looks. This is the slow, compounding part.

## The primary category is the single biggest lever

Your **primary category** does more for relevance than anything else on the profile. Pick the most specific one that describes your core business, not a broad umbrella. "Immigration lawyer" beats "legal services." "Italian restaurant" beats "restaurant." If you get this wrong, you're invisible for the exact searches that matter.

Then add **secondary categories** for everything else you genuinely do - up to nine of them. A beauty salon might add "nail salon," "waxing hair removal service," and "eyelash service." Each one opens a new set of searches you can appear in. Don't stuff in categories you don't actually offer; Google can suspend you for it, and it dilutes your primary signal.

Check what categories your top-ranking competitors use, then match the ones that fit and out-complete them everywhere else.

## The optimization checklist

Relevance rewards completeness. Google favors profiles that are 100% filled in, because a full profile answers more searches. Work through all of this:

- **Business name** exactly as it appears in the real world - no keywords bolted on.
- **Primary + secondary categories** set as above.
- **Full address** (or service area, if you go to clients) and correct map pin.
- **Local phone number**, hours including holidays, and your website URL.
- **Services and products** listed out with short descriptions and prices where it makes sense.
- **Attributes**: wheelchair access, free wifi, women-owned, online appointments, languages spoken. These feed filters people search by.
- **Photos** - the profiles that convert have real exterior, interior, team, and work photos. Add new ones regularly; Google reads activity as a live signal, and listings with more photos get more clicks and direction requests.
- **Google Posts** - short updates, offers, events. They expire, so post something every week or two to keep the profile active.
- **Q&A** - seed the common questions yourself and answer them, before a stranger posts a wrong answer for you.

None of this is one-and-done. The profiles that climb are the ones someone actually tends to.

## Reviews: the biggest lever after categories

After categories, **reviews are the strongest thing you control**. Not just the star rating - the count, how fresh they are, and the words inside them. Google reads review text, so a client who writes "great immigration lawyer in Warsaw" is handing you relevance for that exact phrase.

What actually works:

- **A steady flow beats a burst.** Twenty reviews trickling in over three months looks natural and keeps you climbing. Forty overnight looks bought and can get them filtered. Ask every happy client, right after the job, with a direct link to the review form.
- **Reply to every review, good and bad.** Google confirms responding to reviews is a positive signal, and it shows prospects you're present. In your replies, naturally drop the service and city: "Thanks for trusting us with your residence permit case in Warsaw." That's keyword-rich without being spammy.
- **Handle the bad ones calmly.** A measured public reply to a one-star review often reads better to future clients than a wall of flawless fives.

This is the lever most businesses ignore, and it's exactly why they sit in position 8 while a worse competitor with 90 reviews sits in the pack.

## NAP consistency and your website

**NAP** stands for Name, Address, Phone - and it has to be identical everywhere it appears online: your website, your profile, directories, social pages, industry listings. When Google finds the same details phrased the same way across many trusted sources, it trusts your business more, and that feeds prominence. Mismatched addresses or three different phone numbers do the opposite.

Your website is the anchor of all this. A Google Business Profile pointing at a fast, real website with a matching address, a local phone number, embedded map, and clear service pages sends a far stronger signal than a profile floating on its own. If you serve one city, a dedicated **local landing page** for that city, linked from the profile, ties everything together.

## Mistakes that get you filtered or suppressed

These are the ones that quietly cost people rankings, or get the whole profile pulled:

- **A fake or virtual address.** Using a mailbox, a coworking desk you don't staff, or a home you can't verify is the fastest way to a suspension. Google runs spam sweeps on this.
- **Keyword-stuffing the business name.** Turning "Kowalski Law" into "Kowalski Law - Best Immigration Lawyer Warsaw Cheap" violates the guidelines. Competitors report it, and Google removes the extra words or suspends you. Use your real name only.
- **Review gating.** Filtering for only happy clients, or only asking five-star customers, breaks Google's policy and can strip your reviews. Ask everyone.
- **Buying reviews or posting fakes.** Google's filters catch bursts from empty accounts, and you lose them plus trust.
- **A thin profile.** Empty categories, no photos, no services - you simply won't rank against someone who filled it all in.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to rank higher on Google Maps?**
Basic fixes like the correct primary category and a completed profile can show movement in two to four weeks. Reviews and prominence build over months. It's faster than organic SEO because there's less competition inside a local radius, but it's not instant - a steady flow of reviews is what keeps you climbing.

**What's the single most important ranking factor I can control?**
Your primary category, followed closely by reviews. Proximity outweighs both, but you can't change your address, so the category and a steady review flow are where you win. Get the most specific primary category, then out-review your competitors.

**Do I need a website to rank in the local pack?**
You can rank without one, but a fast website with a matching address, local phone, and service pages meaningfully strengthens your profile through prominence and NAP consistency. Profiles linked to a real, quick site outrank profiles floating alone, especially in competitive cities.

**Can I put keywords in my business name to rank better?**
No. Using anything other than your real-world name violates Google's guidelines. Competitors report it, and Google removes the extra words or suspends the profile. Put keywords in your services, description, posts, and review replies instead - that's allowed and it works.

**How many reviews do I need to get into the local pack?**
There's no fixed number; it's relative to your competitors in your area. Look at who's in the pack for your searches, count their reviews, and aim to pass them with a steady, natural flow. Freshness and keyword-rich text matter as much as the raw count.

**Why does my ranking look different from my competitor's?**
Because proximity is personal. Google ranks Maps results based on where each searcher is standing, so there's no universal position 1. You and a competitor two streets over will each rank higher for people nearby. Track your ranking from the area you actually serve, not from home.
