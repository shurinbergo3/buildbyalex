Manufacturers often treat their website as a formality: an "about us" page, a photo of the workshop, a phone number and a catalogue as one twenty-megabyte PDF. That works precisely as long as customers arrive through referrals. The moment you need new markets or exports, it turns out you cannot be found - not in search, and not through the AI assistant a buyer now consults first.

Here is how a manufacturer's site should be built so it generates enquiries.

## Who searches, and how

Industrial B2B buying works differently from retail. Several people make the decision, the cycle is long, and most of the work happens before anyone contacts you.

**The engineer or technologist** searches by parameters: material, dimensions, tolerance, power rating, standard, temperature range. They need specifications, drawings and documentation.

**The buyer** searches for a supplier: lead times, minimum order quantity, terms, certifications, delivery geography, reliability.

**The manager** checks the company: how old, what facilities, which customers, any references.

Your site has to answer all three, and usually answers none.

## The core problem: everything is in a PDF

Worth its own section, because it happens almost every time.

The product catalogue is a single file. Technical specifications live inside it. Certificates are scans. The result: search engines do not index the parameters, an engineer cannot find you by a specific spec, and the language model a buyer consulted has no idea you exist.

**What to do:** every product line or item needs its own page with specifications as text, in a table. The PDF stays as a downloadable attachment rather than the only carrier of information. That single change usually delivers more than any redesign.

## What the site must contain

### A catalogue with parameters

A page per product group or item, with a specification table, applications, available variants and materials. A parameter filter if the range is large. `Product` markup with technical properties, so both search engines and AI assistants can read it - covered in [the Schema.org article](/en/blog/schema-markup-for-ai-search-2026).

### Downloadable documentation

Datasheets, drawings, certificates, declarations of conformity, manuals, design files. Without registration: demanding contact details before a download blocks more than it gains.

### Manufacturing capabilities

What equipment, which technologies, what tolerances, maximum dimensions, volumes. That is how an engineer judges whether you can handle their job. It is usually the most undervalued page on the site.

### Commercial terms

Minimum order quantity, lead times, logistics, delivery geography, payment terms, made-to-order and build-to-print work. This is exactly what a buyer looks for, and missing information reads as "not our supplier".

### Certificates and standards

ISO, industry certifications, markings, regulatory conformity. For EU export this is often the first filter.

### Industries and applications

Separate pages per segment: who you manufacture for and which problems you solve. Both SEO entry points and a way to show you understand the specifics.

### Delivered projects

With parameters, timelines, the problem and the outcome. Without client names where confidentiality applies - but with specifics.

### An RFQ form that captures the technical brief

Not "name and phone" but fields for the job: part type, material, quantity, drawing or file, timeline, requirements. That saves two rounds of correspondence and immediately tells your team whether the enquiry deserves their time. An [AI agent](/en/services/ai-agents) works well here too: it collects the same information conversationally, answers catalogue questions and hands over a complete enquiry.

### Contacts by function

Sales, technical support, export. One generic address for the whole site means lost enquiries.

## Languages

For a Polish manufacturer working on export, the minimum is Polish, English and German. Ukrainian and Czech get added for specific markets.

What matters is not just translating but doing it correctly: separate URLs per language, correct hreflang, translated specifications and documentation. Covered in [the multilingual website article](/en/blog/multilingual-website-for-business-in-poland). Without that, language versions compete with each other, and you will be absent from German-language AI answers entirely - a model answers in the language of the question and leans on sources in that language.

## SEO for a manufacturer

The specific thing here is that queries are long and precise, competition on them is modest, and each query is valuable.

**What works:** parametric queries (part type plus material, size, standard), "manufacturer of X in Poland", "X made to order", "X to customer drawings", industry phrasings, searches by designation and standard.

**What does not work:** general words about quality and reliability.

Practical conclusion: **the more specific the page, the better**. A separate page for each significant product group and each application beats one large "Products" page.

## How this connects to AI search

A buyer looking for a supplier in a new country increasingly starts by asking a language model: "who manufactures X in Poland". The model answers from what it could read. A company whose specifications live only in a PDF and whose site carries generalities does not make it into that answer.

## FAQ

**Why does a manufacturing company's website bring no enquiries?**
Most often because product specifications exist only in a PDF catalogue while the pages themselves carry generic statements about quality. Search engines do not index parameters inside files, an engineer cannot find you by a specific spec, and a language model knows nothing of your products. The second most common cause is missing commercial information: minimum order quantity, lead times, delivery geography.

**Do I need a catalogue on the site if I already have a PDF?**
Yes, and it is the highest-impact change you can make. The PDF stays as a download, but every significant product group needs a page with specifications as text and in a table, applications and available variants. That makes you findable on the parametric queries engineers actually type, and lets AI assistants cite your data.

**Should documentation sit behind a form?**
In most cases no. Demanding contact details before a datasheet download blocks more potential customers than the contacts it produces: an engineer at the selection stage simply moves to whoever offers the file immediately. Capture contacts where the person is already engaged: in the technical enquiry form, in a conversation with the bot, at the quoting stage.

**Which languages does a Polish manufacturer need?**
Polish and English as a minimum, plus German for the German market. Ukrainian and Czech get added for specific directions. What matters is not only translating descriptions but doing it technically correctly: separate URLs per language and proper hreflang. Models answer in the language of the question and rely on sources in that language, so without a German version you will not appear in German-language answers.

**What should an RFQ form on a B2B site look like?**
It should capture the technical brief, not just a contact: part type, material, quantity, timeline, requirements, and the ability to attach a drawing or file. That saves two or three rounds of correspondence and lets your team assess the enquiry immediately. A good alternative is collecting the same information in a conversation: answering questions one at a time is easier than filling in a long form.
